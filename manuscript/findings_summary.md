# Study Findings Summary — Manuscript v4

**Title:** Terminal Low-Complexity Regions Are Enriched Across All Domains of Life: Evidence from 724 Proteomes Spanning 42 Phyla

**Author:** Stanley Kinnok Chan

---

## Overview

724 proteomes (42 phyla, all 3 domains of life; 929,801 LCRs) were analysed using fLPS 2.0 with a 20-bin positional framework and ≥70% purity SINGLE-type filter. The core question: is the terminal enrichment of low-complexity regions (LCRs) — first shown in Tetrapoda, then in a 94-species invertebrate/protist set — universal across all of life?

---

## Key Findings

### 1. Metazoa: 23/24 phyla confirmed (Table 1)

- Terminal enrichment (14.0–23.1%) is pooled-significant in all 24 tested phyla except **Acanthocephala** (1 sp., 16.4%, p=0.07 — a power limitation, not a biological exception).
- **Six newly tested phyla** all show significant enrichment:
  - Porifera 23.1% | Xenacoelomorpha 21.8% | Tardigrada 19.0%
  - Rotifera 15.9% | Nemertea 20.2% | Collembola 20.3%
- **Porifera** (sponges; pre-neural, ≥650 Mya divergence) establishes this property as ancestrally metazoan.
- **Xenacoelomorpha** extends it to the base of Bilateria.

### 2. All eukaryotic supergroups confirmed (Table 2)

All 16 major eukaryotic lineages tested are pooled-significant:

| Supergroup | Lineage | % Terminal |
|---|---|---|
| Opisthokonta | Fungi | 17.8 |
| SAR–Stramenopiles | Oomycota | 21.2 |
| SAR–Stramenopiles | Bacillariophyta (diatoms) | 17.5 |
| SAR–Alveolata | Apicomplexa | 12.4 |
| SAR–Alveolata | Ciliophora | 24.2 |
| SAR–Rhizaria | Rhizaria | 18.7 |
| Excavata | Euglenozoa | 18.3 |
| Excavata | Metamonada | 18.6 |
| Amoebozoa | Amoebozoa | 16.8 |
| Archaeplastida | Viridiplantae | 24.5 |
| Archaeplastida | Rhodophyta | 13.2 |
| Archaeplastida | Chlorophyta | 11.2 |

Metamonada and Euglenozoa (deepest-branching eukaryotes) show enrichment at 18.6% and 18.3%, placing the origin at or before LECA (~1.5–2 Bya).

### 3. Terminal enrichment extends to prokaryotes

- **Bacteria** (87 spp., 2,706 LCRs): **27.3% terminal**, pooled p~0
- **Archaea** (21 spp., 370 LCRs): **26.2% terminal**, pooled p~0
- Both exceed most eukaryotic lineages in terminal fraction.
- **Length-confound ruled out:** Enrichment increases Q1→Q3 in both domains (Bacteria: 20.6% → 37.0%; Archaea: 19.1% → 35.7%), the opposite of what a length artefact would predict.
- High within-domain CV (Bacteria 50.1%, Archaea 44.7%) reflects low per-species LCR counts; the figure is a domain-level aggregate, not a species-typical value.

### 4. N/C asymmetry is supergroup-specific

A consistent U-shaped positional profile is conserved pan-phyletically, but the N vs. C directional bias differs by lineage:

| Clade | Asymmetry direction |
|---|---|
| Viridiplantae | Strongly **N-terminal** dominant (grasses most extreme: ratio 3.5–4.5) |
| Bacteria (LCR-rich) | **N-terminal** dominant |
| Metazoa, Fungi | Balanced to **C-terminal** dominant |
| Ciliophora, Metamonada, Platyhelminthes | **C-terminal** dominant |

In Viridiplantae, the N-terminal bias follows a phylogenetic gradient: bryophytes ~1.0 → eudicots ~1.9 → grasses ~3.5–4.5, suggesting progressive amplification during land plant evolution, possibly linked to elaboration of the PRT6/PCO N-degron pathway.

### 5. Singleton-LCR proteins drive the signal universally

Proteins with a single LCR show significant terminal enrichment in **41/42** phyla/groups (Acanthocephala excepted, power limitation). Multi-LCR proteins show enrichment in only 23/42 groups. Singleton-LCR proteins are the primary driver across all of life.

### 6. Terminal LCR purity — minority signature in 7 lineages

Terminal LCRs are significantly purer than internal LCRs in only 7 of 42 groups: Apicomplexa, Rhizaria (largest effect, Δ=+0.026), Euglenozoa, Viridiplantae, Annelida, Chelicerata, Echinodermata. The remaining 35 groups, including all prokaryotes, show no significant purity difference — confirming enrichment is primarily **positional**, not qualitative.

## Notable Outliers

| Group | % Terminal | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Apicomplexa | 12.4 | Low due to asparagine-rich internal LCRs (immune evasion in *Plasmodium*); terminal LCRs are significantly purer (Δ=+0.007) |
| Chlorophyta | 11.2 | Driven by *Chlamydomonas* (10.7%); *Ostreococcus* is normally enriched |
| Platyhelminthes | 14.6 | Lowest metazoan fraction; strongly C-terminal biased; mechanistically unexplained |
| Acanthocephala | 16.4 | p=0.07 — 1 species, insufficient power |

## Mechanistic Interpretation

The authors propose a **layered model**:

1. **Universal baseline** — ribosome pausing at start/stop codons and N-terminal processing (N-formylmethionine removal, signal peptide cleavage) generate terminal LCR enrichment in all cellular life.
2. **Eukaryote-specific amplification** — C-degron and N-degron pathways of the ubiquitin-proteasome system reinforce the signal in eukaryotes; N-degron elaboration (PRT6/PCO) drives N-terminal dominance in land plants.
3. **Prokaryote distinction** — N-terminal dominance in bacteria is consistent with N-terminal processing biology (fMet degrons, signal peptides) rather than C-degron biology.

Whether prokaryote terminal LCR enrichment is **ancestral/universal** (LUCA, ~3.8 Bya) or **convergent** with the eukaryotic pattern remains mechanistically uncertain. The next step is identifying which prokaryotic protein classes (secreted, membrane, ribosomal) carry terminal LCRs.

---

## Conclusion

Terminal LCR enrichment is a **near-universal feature of protein architecture** across all domains of life. The signal predates the Last Eukaryotic Common Ancestor (LECA, ~1.5–2 Bya) and likely traces to the Last Universal Common Ancestor (LUCA, ~3.5–4 Bya). Supergroup-specific N/C asymmetry patterns reflect lineage-specific elaborations of a shared baseline mechanism.

## Literature Context & Novelty Assessment

### Prior art — three-way scan (WHY / HOW / WHAT)

| Paper | WHY | HOW | WHAT | Gap left open |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coletta et al. 2010 *(BMC Syst Biol)* | Do LCRs have position-dependent functional roles? | Single proteome (*S. cerevisiae*; 6,165 proteins); SEG; terminal (≤25 aa from end) vs central (≥50 aa); GO + PPI network | LCRs enriched at termini (p=7.6×10⁻⁶); terminal LCRs correlate with PPI connectivity, central with transcription | One organism only; no cross-species or cross-domain testing; no prokaryotes |
| Ntountoumi et al. 2019 *(NAR)* | Are prokaryote LCRs functional or junk? | >1,500 prokaryote + phage proteomes; fLPS + SEG; amino acid composition, GO enrichment, conservation | Prokaryote LCRs are functional, conserved, amino-acid-class-specific (Pro/Gly in bacteria; Thr/Asp in archaea) | Asked WHAT LCRs do, never WHERE in the protein — no positional binning, no terminal analysis |
| Persi, Wolf, Koonin et al. 2023 *(PNAS)* | How do LCRs evolve in prokaryote genomes — adaptive or noise? | Genome-level correlation of per-gene LCR count vs. gene-family paralogy; evolutionary timescale modelling | LCRs and gene duplication are compensatory: LCR formation = rapid short-term adaptation; paralogy dominates long-term | Gene-level evolutionary framing — the positional question (where in the protein?) was never asked |
| Teekas, Sharma & Vijay 2024 *(Open Biology)* | Do LCRs cluster at protein termini, and co-occur with positive selection? | 308 Tetrapoda species, 12 clades; fLPS; 20-bin positional analysis; purity gradient; dN/dS | Terminal regions are LCR hotspots across all Tetrapoda; co-occur with positively selected sites | Tetrapoda only — no invertebrates, no non-animal eukaryotes, no prokaryotes; no N/C asymmetry direction |
| Saravanan, Kravetz & Battistuzzi 2025 *(Front Bioinformatics)* | Are prokaryotic LCRs in plastic accessory genes or conserved core genes? | 3 enterobacteria (*E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Klebsiella*); species + population level; pan-genome core vs. accessory classification | LCRs enriched in core/orthologous genes — contradicts the "tuning knob" evolutionary plasticity hypothesis | 3 species only; never asked positional question; no terminal enrichment analysis |

### Cross-paper synthesis

- **Common WHY:** All papers share the premise that LCRs are undercharacterised as functional, positional, or evolutionary signals — not merely BLAST noise.
- **Divergent HOW:** The positional question (where in the protein?) has only ever been asked in eukaryotes — Coletta (1 yeast proteome) and Teekas (308 vertebrates). Every prokaryote study asked about prevalence, composition, or gene-level evolution — none applied positional binning.
- **Open gap (this paper fills):** *Is terminal LCR enrichment universal across all three domains of life, and what is the N/C directionality of that enrichment?* No published study has asked this.

### What is genuinely new in this manuscript

1. **The prokaryote positional finding (primary novelty).** No published study has shown that LCRs are positionally enriched at the termini of bacterial or archaeal proteins. Ntountoumi 2019 showed prokaryote LCRs are functional; this paper shows they are positionally non-random. The length-stratified control (enrichment increases Q1→Q3, opposite to a length artefact) is decisive.
2. **The N/C asymmetry cross-domain pattern (mechanistic novelty).** Bacteria and land plants are N-terminal dominant; animals and fungi are C-terminal dominant or balanced. This has never been mapped across all of life and is incompatible with a single universal mechanism — which is itself the finding.
3. **The LUCA-level inference (evolutionary novelty).** With 724 proteomes spanning all 3 domains including the deepest-branching lineages (Metamonada, Euglenozoa, Bacteria), the LUCA inference (~3.5–4 Bya) is data-grounded for the first time.
4. **New metazoan phylogenetic anchors** — Porifera, Xenacoelomorpha — secondary to points 1–3 but important for the metazoan chronology.

### Framing problem to fix

The current manuscript is structured as "we did what Teekas did, but bigger." The prokaryote finding is buried in a mid-Results subsection. **Recommended reframe:** lead with the prokaryote result as the primary discovery; reframe the eukaryote/metazoan data as the baseline against which the prokaryote result is measured. One-sentence pitch the paper currently lacks: *"We report the first evidence that terminal LCR enrichment extends to all three domains of life, with a supergroup-specific N/C asymmetry pattern that implies either a universal constraint present at LUCA or independent convergence in prokaryotes."*

## Peer Review — Methodology, Analysis & Sample Critique

### Reviewer 1 (Computational Bioinformatics)

**M1.1 — fLPS parameter choice is not independently validated**
Parameters (min length = 3 aa, purity ≥70%, SINGLE-type) are inherited from Teekas et al. without sensitivity analysis. With a median ~21 LCRs per bacterial species, small parameter changes could substantially alter prokaryote counts in either direction.
*Action: Add a supplementary sensitivity table at three parameter combinations — (current), (length ≥6, purity ≥80%), (length ≥3, purity ≥60%). Confirm enrichment direction is stable.*

**M1.2 — SINGLE-type filter excludes biologically important mixed-composition LCRs**
Mixed-type LCRs (e.g., G+P disordered linkers, E+K mixed segments) are excluded by design. Their positional distribution is unknown. If MULTI-type LCRs show no terminal enrichment, that is itself a finding.
*Action: Run positional analysis on MULTI-type LCR set as supplementary comparison.*

**M1.3 — Pooled prokaryote test ignores phylogenetic clustering**
If 60% of bacterial LCRs come from 3 *Streptomyces* species, the Fisher's exact CI is anti-conservative. The 50.1% CV acknowledges heterogeneity but does not correct for it.
*Action: Apply mixed logistic regression — terminal (0/1) ~ domain + protein-length-quartile + (1|species) + (1|phylum). Alternatively, bootstrap CI by resampling species (not LCRs) within domain.*

**M1.4 — Quartile definition and Q4 reversal not explained**
It is unclear whether protein-length quartiles are defined globally across all bacteria or within each species. The Q4 reversal in bacteria (37.0% → 29.3%) is noted but not investigated — it may be driven by a few large-protein Actinobacteria species.
*Action: State explicitly how quartile boundaries are defined. Add species-colored strip plot showing contribution to Q3/Q4 enrichment.*

**M1.5 — Multiple testing across 42 phyla not corrected**
At α=0.05 and 42 tests, ~2 false positives are expected. Chlorophyta (p=0.025) likely does not survive Holm-Bonferroni correction.
*Action: Apply Holm-Bonferroni across the 42 phylum-level tests; restate Chlorophyta significance honestly in results.*

**M1.6 — Asymmetry ratio is numerically unstable near zero**
pct_nterm / pct_cterm is inflated when pct_cterm is near zero (e.g., *Chlamydomonas*). Single-species phyla with few C-terminal LCRs can show extreme ratios by chance.
*Action: Replace with log-odds asymmetry — log((pct_nterm + 0.5)/(pct_cterm + 0.5)) — for all statistical analyses. Report ratio alongside for interpretability.*

### Reviewer 2 (Evolutionary Biology)

**E1.1 — Choanoflagellates absent — the critical metazoan outgroup**
*Monosiga brevicollis* and *Salpingoeca rosetta* (UniProt reference proteomes) are the unicellular sister to all Metazoa. Their asymmetry direction would directly show whether C-terminal dominance predates animal multicellularity.
*Action: Add ≥2 choanoflagellate species and report N/C asymmetry relative to Porifera and Metazoa.*

**E1.2 — Asgard archaea absent — the closest prokaryote relatives of eukaryotes**
With only 21 archaeal species and no Asgard archaea (Heimdallarchaeota, Lokiarchaeota), the most informative comparison for the eukaryote-origin story is missing. Asgard N/C asymmetry direction would discriminate inherited vs. de novo eukaryotic terminal enrichment.
*Action: Add 3–5 Asgard archaea (available in NCBI); report N/C asymmetry.*

**E1.3 — Plant gradient claim needs PGLS, not description**
"Progressive amplification of N-terminal bias from bryophytes to grasses" is stated narratively. With 118 species and known phylogeny, this is directly testable.
*Action: Fit PGLS regression of asymmetry ratio on phylogenetic distance from bryophytes across the 118 Viridiplantae. Report β coefficient and p-value.*

**E1.4 — Single-species phyla are data points, not phylum confirmations**
Eight phyla have n=1. The abstract names Xenacoelomorpha and Nemertea as supporting pan-metazoan enrichment, but one species of Xenacoelomorpha is not a phylum confirmation.
*Action: Distinguish single-species phyla from multi-species confirmations in abstract/results language. Flag Xenacoelomorpha (Hofstenia) specifically — its phylogenetic position remains debated.*

**E1.5 — Fungi undersampled for the C-terminal dominance claim**
Only 10 fungal species support the "balanced-to-C-dominant" Fungi claim. Microsporidia (highly genome-reduced obligate intracellular fungi) would test whether terminal enrichment persists under extreme genome reduction.
*Action: Expand fungi to ≥20 species including ≥2 Microsporidia (e.g., Encephalitozoon cuniculi, Nosema ceranae).*

**E1.6 — LUCA inference overstated**
Scenario B (convergence) is equally viable per the Discussion, yet Scenario A (LUCA) is favoured in the abstract and conclusions. High bacterial CV (50.1%) means many individual bacterial species show no enrichment; the pooled result reflects an LCR-rich minority.
*Action: Soften LUCA inference to "consistent with a LUCA-level origin" and present Scenarios A and B as equally supported in abstract + conclusions.*

### Reviewer 3 (Molecular & Cell Biology)

**B1.1 — Signal peptide hypothesis is testable within existing data and should be tested**
The Discussion proposes bacterial N-terminal signal peptides could explain N-terminal LCR enrichment. UniProt annotations include SignalP predictions for most reference proteomes. The test: do signal-peptide-containing proteins have higher N-terminal LCR fractions than cytoplasmic proteins?
*Action (high priority): Stratify bacterial LCRs by protein class (signal peptide present vs absent) using UniProt subcellular location annotations. Report N-terminal LCR fraction per class.*

**B1.2 — Amino acid composition of terminal LCRs not compared across domains**
The paper reports C, E, K enriched at metazoan termini. What amino acids dominate terminal LCRs in bacteria (where Pro/Gly dominate per Ntountoumi 2019)? Convergent vs divergent amino acid composition would directly inform mechanism.
*Action: Add per-domain amino acid composition table for terminal LCRs (Bacteria vs Archaea vs Viridiplantae vs Metazoa). This is the most mechanistically informative analysis currently missing.*

**B1.3 — GO enrichment gap is a significant weakness**
Functional interpretation rests on 5 of 723 species. UniProt GO terms (available for most reference prokaryote proteomes, bypassing BioMart entirely) would extend functional analysis to ≥20 bacterial species at minimal cost.
*Action: Extract UniProt GO annotations for LCR-containing proteins in all reference prokaryote species; test enrichment.*

**B1.4 — fMet-degron mechanism cited uncritically**
fMet is co-translationally cleaved from most bacterial proteins by methionine aminopeptidase (MAP); the Piatkov et al. pathway targets only Met-retained proteins. The N-end rule in bacteria (ClpS/ClpAP) recognises L, F, W, Y, R, K — not typical LCR-enriched residues. The connection between fMet-retention and LCR positioning is asserted but not demonstrated.
*Action: Soften fMet degron as speculation, or test directly: do proteins predicted to retain fMet (residue 2 is large/charged, blocking MAP) show higher N-terminal LCR fractions than Met-cleaved proteins?*

**B1.5 — Phase separation biology is not engaged despite strong field relevance**
LCRs are central to liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) and condensate nucleation. Terminal LCR "tails" may serve as condensate-nucleating sequences. PLAAC (already cited) or catGranule scores on terminal vs internal LCRs in model organisms would test this directly and connect to the most active current research front in the field.
*Action: Run PLAAC scores on terminal vs internal LCRs for ≥5 model organisms (yeast, C. elegans, Arabidopsis, E. coli, P. falciparum). Report whether terminal LCRs have elevated LLPS propensity.*
